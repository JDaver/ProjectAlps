# Mathematical Methodology for Transforming a Graph into a 2D Map

## 1. Objective

The objective is to transform the abstract graph generated by the world generation system into a two-dimensional spatial representation, preserving the graph's topological structure as much as possible and subsequently introducing a geometric characterization dependent on the world archetype.

The process is divided into four main phases:

1. **Topological analysis**
2. **Geometric embedding in $\mathbb{R}^2$**
3. **Discretization**
4. **Scaling**

Where:

* $G=(V,E)$ is the graph of regions;
* $D$ is the matrix of topological distances;
* $\mathbb{R}^2$ represents the abstract geometric space;
* $M$ is the discrete matrix representing the map.

---

## 2. Topological Analysis of the Graph

Given a graph with $n$ nodes:

$$
G=(V,E), \qquad |V|=n
$$

the distance matrix is constructed:

$$
D = [d_{ij}]
$$

where $d_{ij}$ represents the minimum number of edges required to reach node $v_j$ starting from node $v_i$.

The matrix is symmetric in the case of an undirected graph:

$$
d_{ij}=d_{ji}
$$

and has zero values along the diagonal:

$$
d_{ii}=0
$$

The distances can be calculated using a **Breadth-First Search (BFS)** starting from each node.

### Centrality

For each node, the following value is defined:

$$
S(v_i)=\sum_{j=1}^{n} d_{ij}
$$

A low value of $S(v_i)$ indicates that the node is topologically central with respect to the graph.

The node

$$
v_c=\arg\min_{v_i\in V} S(v_i)
$$

is therefore selected as the **central node** of the geometric embedding.

This phase uses exclusively topological information and does not yet consider the spatial position of the nodes.

---

## 3. Geometric Embedding in $\mathbb{R}^2$

The central node obtained from the previous calculation is placed at the origin:

$$
p(v_c)=(0,0)
$$

The first expansion from the central node is the simplest case, since there is no incoming direction yet.

For a node $v_k$ with degree $k$, the available directions are initially distributed uniformly along the circumference:

$$
\theta_i=\frac{2\pi i}{k},
\qquad i=0,\ldots,k-1
$$

Each direction is associated with the vector:

$$
u_i=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos(\theta_i)\\
\sin(\theta_i)
\end{pmatrix}
$$

These vectors do not yet represent coordinates in the final matrix. They represent exclusively **directions in the abstract geometric space**.

The position of a node reached through a given direction is then calculated using:

$$
p(v_j)=p(v_i)+r\cdot u_i
$$

where $r$ represents the geometric distance used during the embedding.

### Direction of Arrival

Starting from the second expansion, each node is reached through an edge originating from a previously positioned node.

The direction associated with the incoming edge is considered an **already occupied direction**.

Let $v$ be the current node and $v_p$ the node from which it was reached.

The incoming direction is:

$$
u_{\text{in}}=
\frac{p(v)-p(v_p)}
{\left\|p(v)-p(v_p)\right\|}
$$

The direction of the edge leading back to the previous node, observed from the current node, is therefore:

$$
u_{\text{back}}=-u_{\text{in}}
$$

This direction is not assigned again to a child node.

The remaining $k-1$ directions are instead calculated as a function of the incoming direction and the number of edges still available.

In this way, the distribution of directions becomes **local to each node**: the geometry is no longer determined exclusively with respect to the global axes, but also takes into account the direction from which the node was reached.

### Node Assignment During Expansion

Position assignment and direction assignment take place simultaneously.

When a node is expanded, the adjacent node corresponding to each available direction is first identified.

If the node has not yet been positioned, it is immediately assigned the corresponding position:

$$
p(v_j)=p(v_i)+r\cdot u_j
$$

In this way, each direction is directly associated with a specific neighbouring node, simultaneously establishing its position in space.

Once the expansion of the current node is complete, the algorithm selects, among the child nodes that have not yet been expanded, the one with the lowest centrality value:

$$
v_{\text{next}} =
\underset{v \in C}{\arg\min}\; S(v)
$$

where $C$ contains only nodes that have not yet been processed.

The expansion therefore proceeds recursively according to the structure of the graph, progressively prioritizing the nodes that are topologically more central.

Throughout the embedding phase, every node maintains a continuous position in $\mathbb{R}^2$, independently of the future discrete grid.

A particularly critical case to consider during implementation is the presence of **cycles** in the graph, which will introduce additional complexity into the algorithm.

---

## 4. Archetype-Dependent Transformation

The geometry generated in the previous phase is modified according to the world archetype through a linear transformation:

$$
T:\mathbb{R}^2\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^2
$$

represented by the matrix:

$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
a & b\\
c & d
\end{pmatrix}
$$

The position of each node is transformed according to:

$$
p'(v)=A\,p(v)
$$

In this way, the topological structure of the graph remains unchanged, while its spatial distribution can be geometrically deformed.

For example, for an environment characterized by predominantly vertical development, the following transformation could be used:

$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
\alpha & 0\\
0 & \beta
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\alpha<1,\quad\beta>1
$$

The transformation therefore compresses the horizontal dimension and expands the vertical dimension.

It is important that the transformation is applied to the **geometry** and not to the topology: the graph $G$ remains unchanged.

---

## 5. Geometric Validation

Once the embedding has been completed and the archetype-dependent transformation has been applied, the validity of the configuration is verified.

In particular, the following conditions are checked:

* overlaps between nodes;
* minimum distances between regions;
* possible conflicts in the representation of connections;
* consistency of the generated positions.

Any collisions must be handled through a deterministic repositioning procedure.

This phase is performed in the abstract geometric space, before conversion into the discrete matrix.

---

## 6. Discretization of the Geometric Embedding

The matrix is constructed **only after the geometric embedding has been completed and the validity of the node arrangement has been verified**.

Up to this point, every node is represented by a continuous position in the plane:

$$
p(v_i) = (x_i, y_i) \in \mathbb{R}^2
$$

These coordinates do not yet correspond to cells in a matrix. They represent the geometric positions of the nodes in the abstract spatial domain.

Once the embedding has been completed, the minimum rectangular area containing all nodes is determined.

The minimum and maximum coordinates are:

$$
x_{\min} = \min_i x_i
$$

$$
x_{\max} = \max_i x_i
$$

$$
y_{\min} = \min_i y_i
$$

$$
y_{\max} = \max_i y_i
$$

The resulting bounding rectangle is therefore:

$$
[x_{\min}, x_{\max}]
\times
[y_{\min}, y_{\max}]
$$

This rectangle defines the spatial extent that must be represented by the discrete matrix.

### Coordinate Translation

Before converting the continuous coordinates into matrix coordinates, the entire embedding is translated so that the minimum coordinates become the origin.

For each node $v_i$, the translated coordinates are:

$$
x'_i = x_i - x_{\min}
$$

$$
y'_i = y_i - y_{\min}
$$

This guarantees that:

$$
x'_i \geq 0
\qquad
y'_i \geq 0
$$

for every node.

### Conversion to Matrix Coordinates

The translated continuous coordinates are then converted into discrete matrix coordinates.

The row index is obtained from the vertical coordinate:

$$
r_i = round(y'_i)
$$

The column index is obtained from the horizontal coordinate:

$$
c_i = round(x'_i)
$$

Equivalently:

$$
r_i = round(y_i - y_{\min})
$$

$$
c_i = round(x_i - x_{\min})
$$

where `round` denotes rounding to the nearest integer.

At this point, every node $v_i$ is associated with a discrete matrix position:

$$
M[r_i,c_i] = v_i
$$

The matrix therefore represents a **discrete version of the continuous geometric embedding**.

The matrix is not used during the embedding phase and does not determine the positions of the nodes. It is constructed only after the continuous embedding has been completed and validated.

This separation ensures that the geometric embedding remains independent of the final matrix resolution and representation.

## 9. Future Development

Once the abstract matrix has been obtained and scaled, strategies will need to be defined to transform the initial representation, consisting of a partially empty set of cells identifying the positions of the regions, into a complete spatial map.

This phase may involve different **region-filling strategies**, such as *flood fill* algorithms or other techniques to be defined.

In parallel, it will be possible to introduce **neutral zones** between different regions, which can be used to represent natural boundaries or separations.

These zones may subsequently be interpreted by the terrain generation system as constraints or landscape features. For example, they could be associated with specific *noise* conditions or other procedural functions, allowing the generation of impassable mountain ranges, gorges, rivers, cliffs, or other terrain features.

This phase is deliberately kept separate from the embedding algorithm and constitutes a subsequent level of map processing.
